Months of \"parity\" results hid all of this for two reasons. Most study games used precon decks where the combo code never ran, partly because of a card-name bug. And every change was scored on overall win share, where a gain of about 18 points on Oracle decks and a loss of about 7 on the rest average out to zero. Some study decks were also missing cards: Forge silently dropped 48 double-faced card slots, including the Ral deck's commander, so the conclusion that \"Forge can't play storm\" was never a fair test. The product then tells users a combo is \"AI can fire this; results meaningful\" whenever the deck won later by any means, which mostly meant engines that won by combat a round or more later. The order of fixes that follows from the evidence: first fix the measurements (combo conversion rate, per-deck results, a test that starts games with a combo already on the board); then rebuild the combo data so each line names its finisher, order and targets; then add a small step-runner in the shim that plays those steps through Forge's own rules checks; then let the shim re-admit the cards Forge refuses and fix what tutors can reach and when they are cast. Combat tuning should stop until those land. What nobody knows yet is how much win rate a working step-runner buys, and the seeded-board test can answer that cheaply before anything big is built."
